> The Brown Berets' rise and fall. Origins, 1966 to 1967: the Brown Berets emerge in East Los Angeles, California, from a youth organization originally called Young Citizens for Community Action. It was reorganized as the Young Chicanos for Community Action and formalized as the Brown Berets in 1967, with David Sanchez and Carlos Montes among its early leaders. They developed during the broader Chicano movement as Mexican-American young people challenged discrimination in education, employment, policing, housing, and politics. The Brown Berets adopted the brown beret as a symbol of Chicano identity and resistance and were influenced partly by the organizing style of the Black Panther Party.
>
> Rapid growth: by 1969, the Brown Berets had more than 20 chapters nationwide. Their political program emphasized self-determination in social, political, cultural, and economic liberation. Their activities weren't limited to street demonstrations. They also worked on community programs, including free health care services, educational activism, Chicano ethnic studies advocacy, community organizing, opposition to police brutality, support for students. They helped operate the El Barrio Free Clinic in East Los Angeles from 1968 to 1970.
>
> The East LA walkouts, 1968: one of the most important moments in the rise of Chicano activism was the East Los Angeles walkouts, also called the blowouts. From March the 1st to the 8th, 1968, approximately 15,000 students walked out of seven East Los Angeles schools protesting unequal educational conditions. The Brown Berets supported the students and became increasingly associated with the broader movement for educational equality. The walkouts helped turn Chicano youth activism into a major organized political movement. The Chicano Moratorium, 1970.
