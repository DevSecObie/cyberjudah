## Introduction

*[[13:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yaepTSdYhtg&t=787s)]* The Power Hour from IUIC Phoenix, hosted by Captain Yoshua with officers and soldiers, back after a two-week break: an interview with Dr. Johanna Fernandez, associate professor of history at Baruch College and author of The Young Lords: A Radical History. She tells how a Chicago street gang became a political organization on the Black Panther model, what the Young Lords won for Puerto Ricans and Black Americans in New York, how COINTELPRO and the NYPD watched them, and why that history is hidden. The brothers close by saying what those movements lacked was the Bible as their foundation.

## Class Questions

- **What inspired you to write this book? (19:59)** She started researching the Young Lords as a graduate student at Columbia in the 1990s, when her professors "looked at me like I had three heads: the young who?" She interviewed about 100 people, scoured the archives, and blew all the academy's deadlines because she wanted to write it for an emerging generation. Growing up in the Bronx, she only learned of the Young Lords at an elite university, though they had occupied Lincoln Hospital, where her father almost died in 1970, to call attention to the horrific medical care Black Americans and Puerto Ricans got. Captain Yoshua says he believes he was born in Lincoln Hospital.
- **How did the gang become a political organization? (33:56)** Cha-Cha Jimenez, briefly shown in Judas and the Black Messiah, led a Chicago gang called the Young Lords formed in 1959. In 1968, in prison when Martin Luther King was assassinated, he was radicalized, and on release set out to transform the gang. He met Fred Hampton, and their meeting was the linchpin of Hampton's Rainbow Coalition of Puerto Ricans and Mexicans, Black Panthers, and poor whites from Appalachia. It took the killing of a Young Lord by an undercover cop to politicize the rest. Before that, they had fought white gangs to desegregate beaches and playgrounds in Chicago.
- **What did the Black Panthers bring to the table? (37:25)** A model of organization that worked: political education workshops that transformed people quickly. Cha-Cha was interviewed in the Panther newspaper, Puerto Rican kids in New York read it and went to Chicago to meet him, and he was sent to Oakland to be trained. The Latin American Defense Organization (LADO) helped them publish their newspaper. The Young Lords adapted the Panthers' structure, considered themselves socialists, and started breakfast programs and health clinics. The captain recalls Dr. David Sanchez of the Brown Berets calling it an "exchange of talents" between the groups.
- **Why is this history so obscure? (46:09)** Look at the nervous breakdown over critical race theory. Stripping a people of their history and culture is a strategy of colonial domination, alongside stripping them of land and demonizing them. Texas has a monopoly on what goes into the textbooks used across the country. Captain Yoshua: we've gone over that quite a bit on the show, the demonization and the stripping of identity.
- **What obstacles did you face getting the book out? (48:56)** Finding sources: she turned herself into Inspector Gadget. Most people don't know the first known patient bill of rights was drafted by the Young Lords at Lincoln Hospital, or that they are credited with New York's anti-lead-poisoning legislation: they occupied the health department, went door to door, and found something like 30 of the children they tested lead poisoned. She found the proof in a folder in the city's municipal archive, and found the Young Lords in Mayor John Lindsay's papers too. "Radical" means grasping at the roots: "those are the good guys, not the bad guys."
- **Why did COINTELPRO target the Young Lords? (56:01)** They were self-proclaimed revolutionaries and socialists who believed slavery and capitalism grew up together and produced white supremacy, and that was in line with the times: in 1969 two million students polled believed in building a revolutionary party, and King in 1967 sounded like Malcolm X. Revolutions against colonial rule were in the air, and the Young Lords and Panthers sided with the Vietnamese at the height of the Cold War. COINTELPRO reserved a homicidal campaign for the Panthers; the Young Lords were surveilled and Cha-Cha was followed and arrested until he went underground. The FBI broke bonds of trust among people who loved each other. When the NYPD gave her the runaround on her records request, she borrowed money and sued, and recovered over a million surveillance records of New Yorkers, including Malcolm X's, records a 1985 ruling had told the NYPD to deposit in the municipal archive.
- **What brought the decline? (1:09:20)** She interviewed most of the New York leadership: Juan Gonzalez, Pablo Guzman, Felipe Luciano, Mickey Melendez, Denise Oliver (the first woman in the leadership body, a Black American), and others. COINTELPRO left the Young Lords on its last hair, but movements also ebb and flow, and conservatism rose in the 1970s and 80s, replacing the social safety net with mass prison building. The Young Lords wrote about prisons before academics did, and occupied a church in East Harlem a second time in 1970 after a member was found hanged in a New York jail.
- **What can our people do now? (1:18:11)** Every generation has to develop its own methods. The Panthers' central committee and berets came from Cuba and the third-world liberation movements, and those groups came after more than 15 years of struggle. "The most important thing is to not stand as an individual. You gotta join an organization and you have to build something." The commitment, as the Young Lords put it, is "25/7", which Dr. Sanchez also said; it's not a part-time thing.
- **An officer: what stands out from Cha-Cha Jimenez? (1:27:09)** Integrating the beach. Their parents, new migrants, put their heads down and worked, but the new generation would not bow down: rather than travel far to a beach, they battled the white gangs for the one at the corner, won, and after that their parents could go to the beach too. "It's also about dignity."
- **What about Pedro Albizu Campos and Puerto Rican independence? (1:30:06)** The show had gone through War Against All Puerto Ricans. In his June 7, 1969 Panther interview Cha-Cha said Puerto Rico is a colony and must be freed; Albizu Campos was read and respected in Chicago and New York. The Young Lords were the first to use "Latin" or "Latino" for the people driven here after the U.S. invaded their countries or displaced their farmers. The captain ties it to the Monroe Doctrine, the banana wars, the School of the Americas, the Cuba blockade they saw in 2018, and Juan Gonzalez's Harvest of Empire, and to the demonization of migrants as criminals: "why can't we get people from good countries and not … countries."
- **An officer: what about Puerto Rico today, with Americans buying cheap land for tax breaks? (1:35:42)** It's the colonial project begun in 1898: land displacement, the birth control pill tested on Puerto Rican women, a third of the island's women sterilized. Since Hurricane Maria, Americans are buying property and the youth are leaving; about six million Puerto Ricans live in the diaspora. Under PROMESA the island is about $70 billion in debt and, as a colony, can't file for bankruptcy. The captain calls it gentrification on a mass scale, like the South Bronx. Yet in 2019 the largest uprising in the island's history removed the governor; the question is how to move from episodic uprisings to something sustained.
- **From the audience: why was Cha-Cha your favorite? (1:48:40)** Read chapter one. He has a beautiful sense of humor, he's self-effacing and understated, a leader without ego who wants his people to do better. The Young Lords are remembered because they made common cause with the most persecuted organization in the country, the Black Panthers: as a gang they had no middle-class respectability. Migrants first learn to distance themselves from Black Americans; under Spanish rule a drop of white blood brings you closer to the white ideal, while in the U.S. a drop of black blood makes you black. About 25 to 30 percent of the Young Lords' members were Black Americans. Cha-Cha is sick and not making appearances; she suggests Denise Oliver, Mickey Melendez or Felipe Luciano for the show.
- **Any future projects? (1:56:00)** Making sense of the million NYPD surveillance records from 1954 to about 1972, and what mass surveillance by citizens on each other means for American democracy; she has written an article on the historical roots of American fascism, and sees Trump's rise as the consolidation of the counter-revolution against the 60s.
