# 2 Samuel 17: The Counsel of the Lord Shall Stand

Read the chapter → [[2 Samuel 17]]

## Introduction

This session covers [[2 Samuel 17|2 Samuel 17]] through 20: Ahithophel's counsel against David and Hushai's counter counsel, the great battle in the wood of Ephraim and the death of Absalom, David's mourning and his return to Jerusalem, and the revolt of Sheba the son of Bichri. The thread running through all four chapters is that the devices in a man's heart are many, but the counsel of the Lord, that shall stand. Absalom moved in the spirit of deceit and betrayal against his own father, and the enemies a man of Israel must forgive are the enemies of his own people, even those risen up out of his own house.

- Ahithophel had already given Absalom wicked counsel in the previous chapter, telling him to go in unto his father's concubines in the sight of all Israel so that the hands of all that were with him would be made strong. Ahithophel was David's own counselor turned traitor, being the devil the Bible speaks of, working deceit and betrayal from inside the gates of Israel.
- His counsel in those days was regarded as if a man had inquired at the oracle of God. That tells you Ahithophel was very influential and had the gift of gab, so persuasive that his advice sounded almost biblical.
- Now he asks Absalom to let him choose out twelve thousand men to pursue David that same night while David was weary and weak handed, to smite the king only and bring back all the people. He wanted the victory and the bragging rights of being the one who killed King David.

- Absalom wanted a second opinion, so he called Hushai the Archite to hear likewise what he would say. There is safety in a multitude of counselors, but only where the counsel is according to thus saith the Lord; a counselor who does not go to the scriptures is not one you should want.
- Hushai told Absalom that Ahithophel's counsel was not good at this time. He reminded him that his father and his mighty men were mighty and chafed in their minds as a bear robbed of her whelps, war ready and strategic, and that David would not lodge with the people but move by night.
- Hushai warned that if Absalom's men were overthrown at the first, even the most valiant warrior whose heart is as the heart of a lion would utterly melt, for all Israel knew David was a mighty man. This shows David and his mighty men were on another level; none of Absalom's thousands could compare to them.
- Instead Hushai counseled that all Israel be gathered from Dan to Beersheba as the sand by the sea, with Absalom going to battle in his own person. This deliberately inaccurate counsel would waste time gathering troops and give David room to escape; Hushai was a counterintelligence agent, a spy planted in righteousness, always siding with King David.
- Absalom and all the men of Israel said Hushai's counsel was better than Ahithophel's. The Lord had appointed to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel, to the intent that He might bring evil upon Absalom. The Lord was working with Hushai and David, while Satan was in the spirit of Absalom and Ahithophel.

- The messengers Jonathan and Ahimaaz waited by En-rogel; a maidservant carried the word to them, but a lad saw them and told Absalom. The Most High put His spirit on certain people to orchestrate His plan; often when a man tries to do evil to another, it falls back on him, for he that diggeth a pit shall fall therein.
- They fled to a man's house in Bahurim that had a well, and a woman spread a covering over the well's mouth and ground corn upon it to camouflage it. When Absalom's servants came asking, she told them the men had gone over the brook of water, and the pursuers returned to Jerusalem.
- The two came up out of the well and told David to arise and pass quickly over the water. David and all the people passed over Jordan by the morning light, and there lacked not one of them that was not gone over.

# 2 Samuel 18: Deal Gently With the Young Man

- David numbered the people and set captains of thousands and hundreds, sending a third part under Joab, a third under Abishai, and a third under Ittai the Gittite. He said he would surely go forth with them himself.
- The people answered that he should not go, for he was worth ten thousand of them and it was better that he support them out of the city. David hearkened to their counsel and said what seemed best to them he would do; he did not run a dictatorship. In a time when he could not trust his own flesh and blood, he trusted the proven men he had warred with for years.
- David charged Joab, Abishai, and Ittai to deal gently for his sake with the young man, even with Absalom, and all the people heard the charge. Even after Absalom defiled his concubines and stole the people's hearts, David still sought to make amends with his son.

- The battle was in the wood of Ephraim, and Israel was slain before the servants of David in a great slaughter of twenty thousand men. The wood devoured more people that day than the sword devoured.
- Absalom, riding on a mule, went under the thick boughs of a great oak, and his head caught hold of the oak so that he was taken up between the heaven and the earth while the mule went on. He hung there yet alive.
- A man told Joab he had seen Absalom hanged in an oak. Joab asked why he had not smitten him to the ground for a reward of ten shekels and a girdle, but the man refused, saying he would not put forth his hand against the king's son for a thousand shekels, because he had heard the king charge them to beware that none touch the young man.
- The man told Joab that to have killed Absalom would have been to work falsehood against his own life, for nothing is hid from the king, and Joab himself would have set himself against him. Joab said he would not tarry, took three darts, and thrust them through the heart of Absalom while he was yet alive in the midst of the oak.
- Joab was an evil man with the spirit of murder; he could have brought Absalom back alive for his father to make amends, but he did what he wanted. Ten young men that bore Joab's armor compassed Absalom and smote him and slew him, for a beast will consort to his own like.

- David sat between the two gates while the watchman on the wall saw first Ahimaaz running alone, then another man. The king said a man running alone brings tidings, and he recognized the running of Ahimaaz as a good man coming with good news.
- Ahimaaz fell before the king and said all was well, blessing the Lord who had delivered up the men that lifted their hand against the king; the good news was that David was alive and had overcome. When David asked if the young man Absalom was safe, Ahimaaz said he saw a great tumult but knew not what it was.
- Cushi came and said the enemies of the king and all that rose against him should be as that young man is. David understood that his son was one of the casualties of war. David's whole life was war, slaughtering giants from his youth up, and now he mourned in heartache for his son.

- The victory that day was turned into mourning for all the people, because they heard how the king grieved for his son, and they got them by stealth into the city as ashamed men steal away when they flee in battle.
- Joab came into the house and told David he had shamed the faces of all his servants who had saved his life and the lives of his sons, daughters, wives, and concubines, because he loved his enemies and hated his friends. Joab said if Absalom had lived and all of them had died, it would have pleased David well. No one else could speak to the king this way, but Joab knew David's skeletons.
- Joab charged David to arise and speak comfortably unto his servants, swearing that if he did not, not one man would tarry with him that night, and that would be worse than all the evil that had befallen him from his youth. Then the king arose and sat in the gate, and all the people came before him.
