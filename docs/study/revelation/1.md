**[Revelation 1:9](/bible/revelation/1#v9)**

> <sup>[9](/bible/revelation/1#v9)</sup> I John, who also am your brother, and companion in tribulation, and in the kingdom and patience of Jesus Christ, was in the isle that is called Patmos, for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesus Christ.

- “I John, who also am your brother, and companion in tribulation, and in the kingdom and patience of Jesus Christ, was in the isle that is called Patmos,” Patmos is a little island in the Aegean Sea, shaped like a seahorse. John of Patmos, also called John the Revelator, John the Divine, John the Theologian, is the John of Revelation, the apocalyptic text forming the final book of the New Testament. By most biblical historians he is considered exiled there as a result of anti-Christian persecution under the Roman emperor Domitian. Anti-Christian meaning what? Anti-Israelites keeping the commandments. Domitian was related to Titus and Vespasian, the younger brother of Titus, and he became emperor; that dude was wicked as hell. Since the Roman era, Christians and historians have considered the writer of Revelation to be the apostle John, John the Evangelist, supposed author of the Gospel of John.
- Early tradition says John was banished to Patmos by the Roman authorities, and it is credible because banishment was a common punishment used during the imperial period for offenses like magic and astrology, and prophecy was viewed by the Romans as belonging to the same category. So Rome considered any type of prophecy in the same category as magic and astrology. Prophecy with political implications like John's in Revelation would have been perceived as a threat to Roman political power and order: Rome had its ten hills and ten allies supporting it, like America today with the ten common markets, so they figured this is against Rome, we have to exile this guy. Three of the islands in the Sporades were places where political offenders were banished. According to Tertullian, John was banished to Patmos after being plunged into boiling oil in Rome and suffering nothing from it: they threw him in boiling oil to kill him and he didn't die. It is said that all in the audience of the Colosseum were converted upon witnessing this miracle, in the late first century during the reign of Domitian.
