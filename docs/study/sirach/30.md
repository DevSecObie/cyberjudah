## Introduction

These notes gather what the CyberJudah classes taught on Ecclesiasticus 30 in their scripture breakdowns: verses 1-13, 15-17, 21-25, each with the class's breakdown and the precepts read with it.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:1-3](/bible/sirach/30#v1)**

> <sup>[1](/bible/sirach/30#v1)</sup> He that loveth his son causeth him oft to feel the rod, that he may have joy of him in the end.
>
> <sup>[2](/bible/sirach/30#v2)</sup> He that chastiseth his son shall have joy in him, and shall rejoice of him among his acquaintance.
>
> <sup>[3](/bible/sirach/30#v3)</sup> He that teacheth his son grieveth the enemy: and before his friends he shall rejoice of him.

- “He that loveth his son causeth him oft to feel the rod, that he may have joy of him in the end.” That is correction. If you love your child you will correct him, to save his life when he gets older. “He that chastiseth his son shall have joy in him”, because you guided him right.
- “He that teacheth his son grieveth the enemy”. Esau hates when we teach our children. The SPLC says the weddings and the unity we bring to the community are hatred, and some of our people foolishly listen to the SPLC and the Anti-Defamation League.
- Back then children got a whipping. We integrated and wanted to start disciplining our kids like Esau. No, discipline your kids the way God said to. And it is not the crazy, extreme stuff.
- “He that loveth his son causeth him oft to feel the rod”, the rod of correction and rebuke, “that he may have joy of him in the end.” So when he grows up he is not a drug dealer; he has a sense of morals and the fear of God.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:4-6](/bible/sirach/30#v4)**

> <sup>[4](/bible/sirach/30#v4)</sup> Though his father die, yet he is as though he were not dead: for he hath left one behind him that is like himself.
>
> <sup>[5](/bible/sirach/30#v5)</sup> While he lived, he saw and rejoiced in him: and when he died, he was not sorrowful.
>
> <sup>[6](/bible/sirach/30#v6)</sup> He left behind him an avenger against his enemies, and one that shall requite kindness to his friends.

- “Though his father die, yet he is as though he were not dead: for he hath left one behind him that is like himself.” That son is to be like you. “He left behind him an avenger against his enemies, and one that shall requite kindness to his friends.” There is a man who, after his own father died, offered black and Latin young people a hundred dollars to call their father and say they love him. They said, my father don’t like me; but when they called, the father on the other end said, I’m so glad you called, and the relationship that had dwindled away came back.
- “Though his father die, yet he is as though he were not dead: for he hath left one behind him that is like himself.” The son is the resemblance of the father who chastised and admonished him and brought him up: you remind me of your dad; a straight shooter just like him; you talk like him, you look like him; it's like he's still here. Like Simba when Rafiki made him look harder and he saw his father in his reflection and went to take his kingdom back by any means necessary. That is the fire put in a child when he was taught young, given the diligence, the time and the structure.
- “While he lived, he saw and rejoiced in him: and when he died, he was not sorrowful. He left behind him an avenger against his enemies”. That is what we are trying to create in Israel United in Christ. The leaders, captains, bishops and deacons won't sit in those seats forever, so who are we leaving our future to? Our forefathers had to do the same: they wrote these words so we have them today. We know what our father Adam did in his marriage and with his kids, the suffering of burying one son killed by his other son, and all the destruction his and Eve's decisions brought on down the line, and we are still reading it and living it, trying to correct it. “And one that shall requite kindness to his friends.”
- “Though his father die, yet he is as though he were not dead”. It is as though the father did not even die, because his son comes up in his stead. Everything the father had he poured into his son, and now the son is a replica and an example of his father: “for he hath left one behind him that is like himself.” That is what we saw in 2 Maccabees 7; you know that household had a father in it, and the mother was teaching them.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:7](/bible/sirach/30#v7)**

> <sup>[7](/bible/sirach/30#v7)</sup> He that maketh too much of his son shall bind up his wounds; and his bowels will be troubled at every cry.

- Frederick Douglass made a profound statement: ‘It is easier to build strong children than to repair broken men.’ Sisters, I know you got it hard, or you think you got it hard, but it’s easier to build strong children, because they’re moldable. Our job is harder, brothers: as teachers, it’s not that easy to repair broken men. We’re broken mentally, in spirit; you men and you women are broken. We come in with broken minds, conformed to this world, and now we want to be transformed. That takes time, that takes patience, that takes practice, practicing God’s laws in our lives. What is the formula to repair broken men? It’s the same formula you use to build strong children.
- “He that maketh too much of his son shall bind up his wounds”: brothers, you teachers, the same way the woman is not to make too much over her child, don’t make too much over these men and women that are coming in. “And his bowels will be troubled at every cry.” You know when a child trips, the mother goes, and what did the father say? Let that boy cry, let him cry. So likewise when brothers go through their adversity, as teachers we’re not to jump to their defense. They got to go through that. You must cry.
- “He that maketh too much of his son shall bind up his wounds; and his bowels will be troubled at every cry.” Don’t make too much of your kids, as if they can do no wrong. When the boy falls off the bike, let him get up and get back on the bike; pampering him is how a boy turns out a girly boy.
- “He that maketh too much of his son shall bind up his wounds”. That is spoiling the child: he can do no evil, you always protect him from judgment; that ain't my son, you can't talk to my son like that. That is a womanly spirit. You want your son to get correction from different perspectives around him. A parent doesn't see everything his kids do; they are different people around their friends, around different genders and in different scenes: the perfect soldier in this building, winning awards and commanding the young men, who won't take out the trash at home but is first there when an officer asks him to lead. That's why the accountability is on all of us to watch all of our children. “And his bowels will be troubled at every cry.”

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:8](/bible/sirach/30#v8)**

> <sup>[8](/bible/sirach/30#v8)</sup> An horse not broken becometh headstrong: and a child left to himself will be wilful.

- “An horse not broken becometh headstrong: and a child left to himself will be wilful.” A man or a woman left to themselves will be wilful, wilful to commit sin. It’s the same formula.
- “An horse not broken becometh headstrong: and a child left to himself will be wilful.” The child is compared to a horse. The out of control kids in the videos became self-willed because they were left to BET and the internet while the parent was on the phone or on Facebook.
- “An horse not broken becometh headstrong: and a child left to himself will be wilful.” That is the generation we see: kids left to themselves, parents always at work, and when they're home they're not mentally there. The kids are left to their own devices, learning whatever entertains them until they become it.
- Tell the child what he did, explain it, have him explain back why he's being whooped so he gets a conscience, then whoop him: a righteous whooping on the sides, not in the mouth. Don't be hitting them in the head with spoons and throwing dishes; that causes trauma, and they'll put that same trauma on another kid. A father's crossover whooping, the belt going one way and coming back the other with one hand, was a lesson in itself. We laugh at a father whooping his boys in the barbershop, but it is no joy to do. It is fire to the skin to make the child understand that what he's doing will cause him more harm than that fire: to save him from the fire of the police, from the hands of the other nations and their judicial system, and ultimately from the fire the Lord will bring. Those boys were whooped for stealing, and that deserves a whooping. You have to break that habit, because stealing leads to killing, and Hosea 4 shows what the land becomes if these whoopings don't happen. That is part of nurturing; then you send him to his mama so she can love on him and he gets more understanding of why he's in the situation he's in.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:9-11](/bible/sirach/30#v9)**

> <sup>[9](/bible/sirach/30#v9)</sup> Cocker thy child, and he shall make thee afraid: play with him, and he will bring thee to heaviness.
>
> <sup>[10](/bible/sirach/30#v10)</sup> Laugh not with him, lest thou have sorrow with him, and lest thou gnash thy teeth in the end.
>
> <sup>[11](/bible/sirach/30#v11)</sup> Give him no liberty in his youth, and wink not at his follies.

- “Cocker thy child, and he shall make thee afraid”: the same thing with broken spirits, broken minds. “Play with him, and he will bring thee to heaviness”: he’ll bring you to sorrow.
- “Cocker thy child, and he shall make thee afraid”. Cocker means to pamper, coddle, or excessively indulge someone: you make too much of them, and that child becomes your idol. “Play with him, and he will bring thee to heaviness.” In the movie Funny Valentines the church covered for the pastor’s son, who could do no wrong, when he raped women; they sent him away, and 20 years later he came back and started raping women again.
- “Laugh not with him”: don’t joke around so much with your child, just like don’t joke around too much with these men and women that are coming in, “lest thou have sorrow with him”, lest you have sorrow with them, “and lest thou gnash thy teeth in the end.”
- “Laugh not with him, lest thou have sorrow with him, and lest thou gnash thy teeth in the end.” It doesn’t mean you can’t laugh. It means when he is doing wrong, don’t think it’s cute and adorable. It isn’t, and you will be filled with regret.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:12](/bible/sirach/30#v12)**

> <sup>[12](/bible/sirach/30#v12)</sup> Bow down his neck while he is young, and beat him on the sides while he is a child, lest he wax stubborn, and be disobedient unto thee, and so bring sorrow to thine heart.

- “Bow down his neck while he is young”: young in age, and young in spirit for you adults. “And beat him on the sides while he is a child”: now we know we can’t put our hands on you, but we can put you out; we can put some kind of judgment on you. And that’s not to destroy you; it’s meant to build you, to make you recollect where you went wrong. “Lest he wax stubborn, and be disobedient unto thee”: if we don’t do that, you will wax stubborn and become disobedient unto God’s laws, “and so bring sorrow to thine heart.”
- “Bow down his neck while he is young”: don’t wait till he’s 15 or 18. “And beat him on the sides while he is a child”: the sides are the thigh area. “Lest he wax stubborn, and be disobedient unto thee, and so bring sorrow to thine heart.”
- “Bow down his neck while he is young, and beat him on the sides while he is a child, lest he wax stubborn”. Waxing stubborn goes back to Deuteronomy 21, where the son is brought to the gates, and the gates aren't playing with these kids now. But there is a better way than putting them to sleep: sweat equity. Take their liberty and work them at home: the dishes, cutting the yard, the trash, read this book, practice this, watch this so you get experience, and put them to action. Had we taken their liberty and got the sweat equity at home from the beginning instead of letting them run wild, the government wouldn't step in to get it. Teach them to be adults, because once they're out of your house they have to do it anyway, and at least it can benefit them and their nation.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:13](/bible/sirach/30#v13)**

> <sup>[13](/bible/sirach/30#v13)</sup> Chastise thy son, and hold him to labour, lest his lewd behaviour be an offence unto thee.

- “Chastise thy son, and hold him to labour”: we got to hold you men and women to labour, “lest his lewd behaviour be an offence unto thee”, because if we don’t hold you to labour you will become lewd in the congregations. I hope you captains understand what I’m going over.
- “Chastise thy son, and hold him to labour”: give him chores, give him labor, “lest his lewd behaviour be an offence unto thee.” Lewd means crude, offensive, or open in a sexual way, showing or causing lust in a cheap way. If you don’t hold him to labor, lewdness will set in, and he starts playing with himself and doing all kinds of things. That is what God is saying will happen.
- Childhood trauma can affect a marriage and parenting later in life, but these are possible patterns, not destiny: we can be fixed. It is up to every man and woman to see what applies to them, and to handle it before getting married. When courting, ask what kind of family the person comes from. The flip side is that one who went through it can decide, I won’t be how my mother or father was; that is what the Bible does.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:21](/bible/sirach/30#v21)**

> <sup>[21](/bible/sirach/30#v21)</sup> Give not over thy mind to heaviness, and afflict not thyself in thine own counsel.

- When something bad happens you have three choices: you can let it define you, you can let it destroy you, or you can let it make you durable. The three Ds: define, destroy, durable. You have to pick which one.
- When you let bad things define you: “Give not over thy mind to heaviness, and afflict not thyself in thine own counsel.” Sometimes bad things happen and you let that destroy your world, or things do not go the way you planned and you let that define you, and everything is wrong.
- It causes low self-esteem, a negative self-image: negative self-talk, focusing on your flaws and exaggerating imperfections, feeling unworthy or inadequate, avoiding challenges, hesitant to take risks out of self-doubt. When you let a bad situation define you, you do not see yourself in a good light. You run from situations, because you feel you can't do it, you can't overcome, you just ain't right, you're no damn good.
- Again, different ways to strengthen ourselves. “Give not over thy mind to heaviness”: don’t give your mind to heaviness. “And afflict not thyself in thine own counsel.”

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:22](/bible/sirach/30#v22)**

> <sup>[22](/bible/sirach/30#v22)</sup> The gladness of the heart is the life of man, and the joyfulness of a man prolongeth his days.

- “The gladness of the heart is the life of man, and the joyfulness of a man prolongeth his days.”
- “The gladness of the heart is the life of man”: when you are happy, it brings life to you, and some joy brings a longer life. Think of the opposite: why does the husband always drop dead before the wife? He lets stress and worry wear him out into the grave, while she is happy. Do you die from the food, the death in the pot, the high cholesterol, or from stress? People who left America say the sickness left them and the energy came back. This place was created to kill us.
- “The gladness of the heart is the life of man.” That is your medicine. You have to be glad in your heart and fight that spirit, not let it overcome you. “And the joyfulness of a man prolongeth his days.”
- “The gladness of the heart is the life of man, and the joyfulness of a man prolongeth his days”: why? Because you have a healthy heart, which is your mind.

**[Ecclesiasticus 30:23](/bible/sirach/30#v23)**

> <sup>[23](/bible/sirach/30#v23)</sup> Love thine own soul, and comfort thy heart, remove sorrow far from thee: for sorrow hath killed many, and there is no profit therein.

- “Love thine own soul”: love your own soul and do it no harm. “Comfort thy heart, remove sorrow far from thee”: nobody cares you’re going through it; that’s life, time and chance is for all of us. “For sorrow hath killed many, and there is no profit therein.”
- “Love thine own soul”: some of you hate yourselves. Love your soul and take care of your body: walk an hour a day. A brother lost a great deal of weight only by walking around his neighborhood an hour every day and changing his diet. Walk before you go home after a long shift, because once you are in the house you have more excuses than a crackhead.
- “And comfort thy heart, remove sorrow far from thee”: some of you love sorrow. “For sorrow hath killed many”: stress and worry have killed many, and you are no different. Stop worrying about what you cannot change. Food and gas are going up and you are not getting a raise, but when you prepare for what you know is coming, you will not stress over it. You see ten dollars a gallon and you already knew, because you read the scriptures; the Most High God already told you, so your mind is prepared. It is what it is.
- “Love thine own soul, and comfort thy heart.” You must learn to comfort your own heart. And brothers must comfort their wives' hearts too: in postpartum difficulties, and in general, when she is overwhelmed by life and feels down even when it has nothing to do with the kids.
